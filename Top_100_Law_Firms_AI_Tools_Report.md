# Top 100 Law Firms: Legal AI Tool Adoption Report

**Date:** March 2026
**Scope:** Am Law 100 and Global 100 law firms — third-party legal AI acquisitions, proprietary tool development, and the "build-then-buy" phenomenon

---

## Executive Summary

The legal AI market has undergone a dramatic transformation between 2023 and early 2026. Gross revenue across the Am Law 100 climbed 13.3% to $158.3 billion in 2024, and technology spending surged 9.7% — the fastest real growth ever recorded in the legal industry. Nearly 8 in 10 of the top 20 firms have adopted third-party AI tools, while almost half have developed or customised their own systems.

Three third-party legal AI platforms dominate the market:

- **Harvey** — ~1,000+ customers across 60+ countries; majority of Am Law 100 firms; $190M ARR (end 2025); valued at ~$8–11B
- **Legora** (formerly Leya) — 400+ law firms and in-house teams across 16 countries; $265M total funding
- **DeepJudge** — Ranked #1 in 2025 SKILLS.law NPS survey; 500%+ YoY revenue growth; $41.2M Series A

A critical trend has emerged: **several firms that initially built proprietary AI tools on Azure OpenAI or similar infrastructure have subsequently acquired third-party platforms** — acknowledging that purpose-built legal AI products outperform homegrown wrappers for complex workflows like large-scale document review and due diligence.

## 1. Methodology and Definitions

**Firm ranking** is based on the 2025 Am Law 100 (by gross revenue) supplemented by key Global 100 firms (particularly Magic Circle and major international firms) that are significant players in legal AI adoption.

**Definitions:**
- **Third-Party Tool:** A commercial legal AI product licensed from an external vendor (e.g., Harvey, Legora, DeepJudge, CoCounsel, Kira Systems)
- **Proprietary/In-House Tool:** An AI tool built by the firm's own innovation or technology team, typically on infrastructure like Microsoft Azure OpenAI, Google Cloud, or AWS
- **Build-Then-Buy:** Firms that initially developed proprietary AI solutions but subsequently acquired or supplemented with third-party legal AI platforms — the focus area of this report

**Data sources:** Public announcements, press releases, vendor customer pages, industry publications (Artificial Lawyer, Legal IT Insider, LawNext, Law.com), and analyst reports through March 2026.

**Important caveat:** Many firms do not publicly disclose their AI vendor relationships. The absence of a firm from a vendor's customer list does not necessarily mean the firm does not use that vendor. Harvey, for instance, claims a "majority of Am Law 100 firms" as customers but has only publicly named a handful.

## 2. Third-Party Legal AI Vendor Overview

### Harvey
- **Founded:** 2022 (San Francisco)
- **Technology:** Built on Azure AI infrastructure (OpenAI o1-preview, o1-mini, GPT-4, GPT-4 Turbo) plus proprietary fine-tuned models
- **Capabilities:** Legal drafting (#1 ranked), due diligence (#2 ranked), contract analysis, compliance, agentic workflows
- **Scale:** 1,000+ customers, 60+ countries, $190M ARR (end 2025)
- **Funding trajectory:** $3B (Feb 2025) → $5B (Jun 2025) → $8B (Dec 2025) → ~$11B (Feb 2026, reported)
- **Notable moves:** Acquired Hexus (Jan 2026); hired Tara Waters (ex-Ashurst CDO), Farrah Pepper, Joe Marando; brand partnership with Gabriel Macht (Feb 2026)

### Legora (formerly Leya)
- **Founded:** 2023 (Stockholm, Sweden)
- **Technology:** Built on Microsoft Azure; multi-agentic architecture, RAG, large-scale parallelisation
- **Capabilities:** Document review (up to 100,000 documents in parallel), legal research, drafting, workflow automation, tabular review
- **Scale:** 400+ law firms and in-house teams across 16 countries
- **Funding:** $265M total (including $150M round in late 2025); $675M valuation (May 2025)
- **Notable:** Launched "Portal" product for firm-client collaboration (Q1 2026)

### DeepJudge
- **Founded:** Zurich, Switzerland (ETH Zurich AI researchers)
- **Technology:** Enterprise search and retrieval-augmented AI agents; connects to DMS, email, intranet, client portals
- **Capabilities:** Cross-system precision search, knowledge management, AI workflows
- **Scale:** 500%+ YoY revenue growth; $41.2M Series A (led by Felicis, Coatue)
- **Recognition:** #1 Most Recommended AI Vendor in 2025 SKILLS.law NPS survey

### Thomson Reuters CoCounsel
- **Technology:** Agentic AI built on Westlaw content; uses Anthropic Claude, OpenAI GPT, Google Gemini plus proprietary models
- **Scale:** 1 million+ professionals across 107 countries (Feb 2026)
- **Capabilities:** Deep legal research, agentic workflows, document review, contract analysis

### Other Notable Vendors
- **Kira Systems** — Contract review and due diligence (used by DLA Piper, Clifford Chance, Freshfields, Goodwin)
- **Relativity aiR** — E-discovery and document review (Azure OpenAI/GPT-4)
- **Centari, Syntheia, Lega, SimplyAgree, Avvoka, vLex, Draftwise** — Various specialist legal AI tools ranked in the 2025 SKILLS Top 15

## 4. Firms That Built Proprietary Tools Then Acquired Third-Party Solutions

This is the most strategically significant trend in legal AI. These firms invested in building their own AI capabilities — typically on Microsoft Azure OpenAI — only to subsequently license third-party legal AI platforms that proved superior for specific complex workflows.

### 4.1 Linklaters — Built "Laila," Then Adopted Legora

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Proprietary Tool** | **Laila** — GenAI chatbot built on Microsoft Azure OpenAI |
| **Usage** | 200,000+ uses per month across the firm |
| **Third-Party Acquired** | **Legora** — firmwide rollout across all 30 global offices (Sep 2025) |
| **Why the Switch?** | Feedback showed Legora handles larger documents and larger document sets better; praise for tabular review feature used in due diligence |
| **Current Status** | Laila's future development is "under review"; Legora now the primary GenAI platform alongside Laila, ReportIQ, and CreateiQ 2.0 |
| **Key Insight** | Even a well-adopted proprietary tool (200K monthly uses) was supplemented when a purpose-built legal platform demonstrated superior capabilities for complex tasks |

## 7. Key Trends and Analysis

### 7.1 The "Build-Then-Buy" Pattern

The most significant finding is that **firms which built proprietary AI tools are increasingly supplementing or partially replacing them with third-party legal AI platforms**. The pattern is consistent:

1. **Phase 1 (2023-early 2024):** Firm builds internal chatbot/assistant on Azure OpenAI or similar (e.g., Laila, FleetAI, AGPT, CC Assist)
2. **Phase 2 (mid-2024-2025):** Firm discovers limitations of homegrown tool for complex workflows (large-scale document review, multi-step due diligence, tabular analysis)
3. **Phase 3 (2025-2026):** Firm licenses purpose-built legal AI platform (Harvey, Legora, or DeepJudge) to handle complex workflows while often retaining proprietary tool for simpler prompting tasks

**Key examples:** Linklaters (Laila → Legora), Dentons (FleetAI → Legora), Addleshaw Goddard (unnamed tool → Legora), Freshfields (Google Cloud → DeepJudge)

### 7.2 The Multi-Vendor Reality

Firms now average **18 live AI solutions** according to survey data, though actual adoption rates remain low (~20% of lawyers at the largest firms use AI assistants regularly). The trend is toward a multi-vendor stack:

- **General prompting:** Proprietary tool or ChatGPT/Copilot
- **Legal research:** Harvey or CoCounsel
- **Document review/due diligence:** Legora or Harvey
- **Enterprise knowledge search:** DeepJudge
- **Contract review:** Kira Systems or Relativity aiR

### 7.3 Harvey vs. Legora: The Emerging Duopoly

The legal AI market is consolidating around Harvey and Legora as the two dominant platforms, with distinct positioning:

- **Harvey:** Stronger in transactional research, due diligence, legal drafting; US-centric but expanding globally; higher-profile funding ($8-11B valuation)
- **Legora:** Stronger in large-scale document review, tabular analysis, workflow automation; European origin but expanding to US; collaborative model with firms as design partners

### 7.4 The "Wrapper Economy" Under Threat

Both Harvey and Legora face strategic pressure from two directions:
- **Above:** Thomson Reuters and LexisNexis have authoritative content moats and are building their own AI (CoCounsel, Lexis+ AI)
- **Below:** Foundation model providers (Anthropic, OpenAI) are increasingly capable of handling operational legal work directly

Casualties are already emerging: Robin AI peaked at 200+ employees in early 2025 before declining to ~150 and announcing layoffs.

### 7.5 Adoption Gaps

Despite the buzz, significant gaps remain:
- Only ~20% of lawyers at the largest firms regularly use AI assistants
- 4 firms reportedly stopped using Harvey; 9 firms stopped using CoCounsel — citing cost and lack of ROI
- The billable hour model (90% of legal revenue) creates a structural disincentive to deploy productivity-boosting AI at scale
- Some firms report 100x productivity gains on specific tasks, yet none of the Am Law 100 anticipate reducing attorney headcount

### 7.6 Elite US Firms: The Silent Majority

A striking pattern is the **lack of public AI vendor disclosures from the most prestigious US firms**. Firms like Wachtell, Cravath, Sullivan & Cromwell, Davis Polk, Simpson Thacher, and Gibson Dunn — despite being among the highest-revenue and most profitable firms — have not publicly confirmed specific legal AI vendor relationships. This may reflect:
- A deliberate strategy to avoid signalling dependence on specific vendors
- The use of AI tools under NDA/confidentiality arrangements
- A wait-and-see approach given the rapidly evolving landscape
- Concern about client perception regarding AI use in high-stakes matters
